Technique · 3D strata

Stack change through time, layer by layer.

A 3D strata map lifts overlapping shapes into stacked slabs, one level per group: a decade, a year, an event or a category. Where shapes overlap, the slabs pile up, so places that change, burn or flood again and again rise into tall stacks you can read at a glance.

How it works

Three settings make a strata map.

Group the shapes

Choose a field with a few ordered values, such as a decade, a year or a severity class (40 groups at most). Each group gets its own level, lowest value at the bottom. Reverse the stacking order to put the highest at the bottom instead.

Size the slabs

Set the slab thickness, the slot separation between levels and the gap between them. Heights are display distances that keep levels apart; they are not real altitudes. A number field can set each slab's thickness instead.

Colour and play it

Pick a palette or a colour per group under Slab colours. In Cinematic mode, the Strata effect drops each step's shapes onto the stack as the timeline plays, building the history up in order.

Change over time

Shapes that move, grow or shrink.

Strata suit any boundary recorded more than once, where the change is in the shape itself.

Environment

Glaciers and ice

Ice extent by survey year. A retreating glacier reads as a stepped, shrinking stack.

Water

Lakes and reservoirs

Surface extent by year, so drought drawdown and recovery show as layers.

Coasts and rivers

Shorelines and channels

Shoreline or river channel positions over time, for erosion and channel migration.

Cities

Urban growth

City limits or annexations by decade, or built-up area by period.

History

National territory

Borders and territories through time, showing what was held, lost and regained.

Census

Boundaries between censuses

Tract or district boundaries by census year, or redistricting plans side by side.

Land

Deforestation

Clearing by year, such as Brazil's PRODES polygons, showing where loss spreads.

Ecology

Ranges and outbreaks

Species ranges or disease outbreak zones by period, showing shifts and spread.

Infrastructure

Service coverage

Broadband or transit service areas by year, showing where coverage arrived first.

Repeated events

Places hit again and again.

When the same ground is affected by many events, the stack height shows how often it happened.

Categories and real layers

Not only time.

Groups can be categories instead of dates. Stacked footprints show how many layers cover a place; real layered things keep their natural order.

Stacked footprints

Overlapping districts

School, water, fire and tax districts stacked by type, showing how many cover each place.

Hazard zones by severity

Flood return periods (100-year, 500-year) or sea-level-rise scenarios (1 ft, 3 ft, 6 ft).

Airspace

Airspace classes by altitude band. Heights are for display, not true altitude.

Each record is one shape in one group. Data whose values change while the shapes stay the same, such as census counts on fixed tracts, reads better as a colour map with a time slider. Census boundary changes between counts do suit strata.

Example · real layers

Arizona's rocks, oldest at the bottom.

Every rock unit on Arizona's state geologic map is stacked by its oldest possible age, from 1.8-billion-year-old Proterozoic rock at the bottom to Holocene sediment on top. On the Colorado Plateau, the Permian rim rock of the Grand Canyon, the Triassic Painted Desert and the Cretaceous rock of Black Mesa step up in order. The young basin fill of the south rises to the top of the stack. Slabs take their period's colour from the geologic time scale.

Ages that do not sort

Ages come as text such as Paleozoic - Permian - Kungurian, which no alphabetical or number sort puts in time order. The map lists its 33 ages oldest first, with order: "listed", and the stack keeps that order.

Height is order, not depth

Each age gets an equal slot, so a slab's height shows where it falls in time, not how thick or deep the rock is. Units cover the ground once, so the stack reads like terraces rather than piles.

The source

USGS State Geologic Map Compilation (SGMC), filtered to Arizona's 4,000 or so mapped units. Units are not matched across state lines.

Make your own

Turn any polygon layer into strata.

Add a polygon layer, open its Advanced style, tick Show as a 3D chart and choose Temporal strata or Stacked footprints. Tilt the map to see the stack. It works on both map engines.
